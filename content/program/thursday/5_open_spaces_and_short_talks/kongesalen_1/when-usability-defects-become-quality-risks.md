What if a system works as specified, yet still frustrates, misleads, or even manipulates its users?

Traditional testing rightly prioritizes functionality, performance, and security. However, in large public-facing systems, seemingly minor user experience (UX) defects can create significant quality risks. Unclear feedback, misleading messages, unpredictable navigation, and poor visual consistency may not break the software, but they trigger dissatisfaction, increase cognitive load, and may gradually erode trust.

In this experience-based talk, I share a case study from a Norwegian public vehicle portal serving millions of users. Shortly after joining the organization, I explored the system with fresh eyes and no historical assumptions. Exploratory UX testing revealed recurring patterns that had become normalized across services: inconsistent interfaces and terminology, generic error messages masking permission or access problems, different message types being presented in the same context and therefore harder to interpret, and navigation elements appearing in unexpected places, forcing users to abandon familiar interaction patterns.

Individually, these defects were often labelled minor or cosmetic. Collectively, they represented usability debt with tangible consequences, including repeated errors, unnecessary retries and workarounds, greater demand for support, and reduced confidence in the service. When such issues accumulate, functionality may remain correct, but user trust and confidence in the service can deteriorate.

Beyond unintentional UX defects, I will also present examples of so-called “dark patterns” from large digital platforms, where design choices intentionally influence user behaviour in ways that may not serve the user's best interests. This highlights the often-blurred boundary between poor usability and manipulative design, where questions of ethics, transparency, and regulation arise.

This talk challenges the traditional view that UX defects are merely cosmetic and demonstrates how testers can identify, communicate, and prioritize them as genuine quality risks. Attendees will leave with practical techniques, real-world examples, and a new perspective on how UX testing helps uncover hidden quality risks, strengthen user trust, reduce usability debt and drive better quality decisions across teams.
